The problem

A video shows where, not how hard.

Video generators can now dream up a robot doing a task. Turn those frames into a trajectory and a real robot can copy the motion. But a video only shows where the arm goes, not how hard it presses. For wiping, peeling, or pressing a button, that missing force is the difference between finishing the job and hovering a few millimeters above the surface, or slamming into it and triggering a safety stop.

The generated audio fills the gap. Contact sounds get louder as contact gets harder, so we use loudness as a proxy for force. From one task description we generate video and audio, take the motion from the video and the force profile from the audio, and run both on a Franka Panda with a force regulator in the loop. Those executions are good enough to become training data, too.

The pipeline

Four steps from a sentence to contact.

  1. GenerateOne RGB image plus a text prompt goes to Seedance 2.0, which returns a video with audio (the exact prompts are at the bottom).
  2. Motion from videoFind the gripper and object (MolmoPoint), segment them (SAM 2), and track them in 3D (TAPIP3D) to get the end-effector path, the contact moments, and the force direction.
  3. Force from audioIsolate the contact sound (SAM-Audio) and turn its loudness into a force-magnitude profile over the contact interval.
  4. ExecuteA 1 kHz impedance controller follows the path while a force regulator tracks the audio-derived profile during contact.

Sanity check

Does the shape of the force matter?

If any contact force works, we could just press with a constant 10 N and skip the audio. So we tried that. On wiping and peeling we ran the same trajectories with two audio-shaped profiles (peaking at 10 or 15 N) and two constant profiles (10 and 15 N), ten trials each.

Constant force fails at first contact: the command jumps from 0 to 10 N in one step, the measured force overshoots toward the 20 N limit, and the robot stops itself. The audio-shaped profile starts low and ramps up after contact, so it never trips the limit, and it finishes with a lower peak force and less total impulse.

Then train on it

The pipeline is also a data engine.

Every successful execution is a demonstration with images, poses, and a force profile. So we let the pipeline collect 50 demonstrations per task at random object positions and trained a Diffusion Policy on them: two camera views plus the end-effector pose in, poses out. We trained two versions, one that also sees the measured contact force and one that doesn't.

At test time there is no force regulator. The policy just commands poses on new object placements and appearances, and the contact force comes from how deep it learned to press. Without force input it succeeds in 29/40 trials; with force input, 34/40. On most tasks the policy's force stays within about 2 N of the demonstrations. Peeling is the exception: the policies press harder than the demos, and the one with force input presses hardest, which peels more reliably.

Appendix

The prompts we used.

Every generation call is one shared system prompt with the task description dropped into it.

System prompt 
Video: The camera remains stationary while <TASK SPECIFIC PROMPT>.

Audio: Emphasize the sound of physical interaction and contact force central to the action, while suppressing all ambient, background, and unrelated sounds.
Whiteboard wiping
The gripper, holding the eraser, slowly wipes the black marks off the whiteboard from right to left once, then retracts at the end.
Carrot peeling
The left gripper, holding the yellow peeler, slowly peels off a layer of the carrot skin, while the right gripper remains still.
Chocolate box stacking
The gripper's left finger slides fully under the chocolate box, forms a fully stable grasp, and slowly puts it on the pile of books.
Lamp button pressing
The gripper performs a single press on the white button on the table and then rises back up, while remaining closed.

Audio separation uses one SAM-Audio prompt for every task: “The sound of contact between gripper and <object>”.
